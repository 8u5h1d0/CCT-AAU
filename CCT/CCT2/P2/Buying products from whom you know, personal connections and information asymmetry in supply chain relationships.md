# Buying Products From Whom You Know: Personal Connections and Information Asymmetry in Supply Chain Relationships

## Quick Reference Table

| **Concept** | **Definition** | **Measurement** | **Key Finding** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Personal Connection** | Pre-existing relationship between customer and vendor executives | Education (same university, degree, overlap) or Prior employment (same firm before current jobs) | Increases supplier selection probability by 60% |
| **Supplier Selection** | Process of choosing which vendor becomes actual supplier | Binary indicator: 1 if vendor selected, 0 otherwise | 35% baseline probability |
| **Information Asymmetry** | Knowledge gap between customer and vendor | Distance (geographic) or Accounting quality (abnormal accruals) | Personal connections have stronger effect when asymmetry is higher |
| **Connection Types** | Source of personal relationship | Educational (7.4%), Past work (16.3%), Combined (21%) | Past work connections show larger effect than educational |
| **Executive Ranks** | Level of connected executive | CEO, C-Level, COO, CFO, Non-C-Level | C-Level connections stronger; COO connections strongest |
| **Contract Restrictiveness** | Provisions imposing costs/penalties on supplier | Index (0-6): warranty, insurance, inspection, meetings, certification, guarantee | Personal connections reduce restrictiveness by 0.74 provisions |
| **Operating Efficiency** | Customer performance after supplier relationship | Inventory volatility, COGS volatility, Sales/Assets, ROA | All four measures improve with connected suppliers |

**Key Variables:**
- **CONNECTED** : Indicator = 1 if educational or work-based connection exists, 0 otherwise
- **SUPPLIER** : Indicator = 1 if vendor is selected as supplier, 0 if non-supplier vendor
- **DISTANCE** : $\ln(\text{miles between headquarters})$
- **RESTRICTIVENESS** : Number of restrictive provisions in contract (max = 6)

## 1. Introduction

### The Critical Decision of Supplier Selection

This study investigates the effect of personal connections on a key corporate decision: **supplier selection**. Selecting suppliers represents a critical element of a firm's supply-chain management and ultimately of its success.

>[!important] Growing Complexity in Supplier Selection
>Supplier selection has become more complex in recent years as more firms outsource larger parts of their supply chain. This trend accentuates the information-asymmetry problem in supplier selection, as customers must rely increasingly on qualitative measures (reputation, management quality, delivery reliability) rather than just price when evaluating vendors.

### The Information Asymmetry Problem

The rise of outsourcing in the supply chain and the need to rely on qualitative supplier-selection measures create significant information-asymmetry challenges. The literature on supplier selection specifies several qualitative measures as prominent in evaluating vendors:

**Top Supplier Selection Criteria (by rank):**
1. **Product quality and reputation** in product market
2. **Delivery reliability / on-time delivery**
3. **Price / cost competitiveness**
4. **Manufacturing capability**
5. **Management quality**
6. **Technology / technical capability**
7. **Research and development**
8. **Financial strength / risk**
9. **Production flexibility / responsiveness**

>[!note] Why Qualitative Measures Matter
>Research (Ho et al. 2010) shows that product quality, reputation, and delivery reliability rank first and second among evaluation criteria in supplier-selection literature, whereas price/cost ranks only third. This emphasis on qualitative factors accentuates the importance of soft information and informal channels of acquiring information by customers.

### Personal Connections as an Information Channel

Because research suggests personal connections can mitigate information asymmetries in investing and financing decisions (Cohen et al. 2008; Duchin and Sosyura 2013; Engelberg et al. 2012; Fracassi 2017; Gao et al. 2014), this study focuses on the role personal connections play in supplier selection.

>[!info] How Personal Connections Reduce Asymmetry
>McCarthy et al. (2013) note that low information asymmetries between supply-chain partners are achieved when "both parties are more inclined to trust each other, communicate more openly, and coordinate and collaborate for mutual benefits." If personal connections can foster trust and open communication to mitigate information asymmetry between potential trading partners, their presence is likely to increase the likelihood of formation of supply-chain relations.

### Identifying Personal Connections

**Data Source:** BoardEx database reports:
- Universities executives attended
- Employment histories
- Affiliations with charitable/volunteer organizations
- Board of directors service

**Connection Types Studied (to address reverse causality):**

1. **Educational Connections**: Same university, same degree program, at least one-year overlap
2. **Prior Employment Connections**: Worked at same firm before current jobs at supply-chain partners

>[!warning] Addressing Reverse Causality
>This study is mindful of potential reverse causality in which business relationships may lead to personal connections. Therefore, following Engelberg et al. (2012), the focus is on personal connections that **preceded business relations by years or even decades**—formed when executives were enrolled in same degree program or worked together before their current jobs.

### Research Design: Identifying Non-Supplier Vendors

To analyze the economic effect of connections on supplier selection, the study:

**Step 1:** Compiles first-time actual supplier-customer pairs from Compustat Segment file (2000-2011)

**Step 2:** Identifies vendors that same customer considers alternatives to chosen supplier

>[!example] Why Focus on First-Time Relations?
>**Two reasons:**
>1. **Mitigate reverse causality**: Repeated business may foster formation of personal connections
>2. **Reduce serial correlation**: First-time relations less prone to statistical dependence issues

**Challenge:** Actual vendors customer considers in vetting process are unobservable

**Solution:** Following Erel et al. (2019), use **revealed preferences** and **Mahalanobis distance method** to identify two vendors closest in relevant observable attributes:
- Four-digit SIC code (same industry)
- Firm size (total assets)
- Sales
- Profitability (ROA)

>[!tip] Robustness to Matching Choices
>The study conducts batches of robustness tests by:
>- Altering attributes matched on
>- Changing number of vendors matched with selected supplier
>- Using alternative industry classifications (text-based instead of SIC)
>
>Results are robust to these choices.

### Key Findings Overview

**Main Result:**

The likelihood of a vendor becoming a supplier increases if customer and vendor executives are personally connected. This result holds for both educational and prior employment connections.

- **Magnitude**: Existence of personal connections increases likelihood of selection by **60% of baseline probability** (21 percentage points on 35% baseline)

**Endogeneity Mitigation Strategies:**

1. **Cross-sectional variation by executive rank/position**: C-Level connections exhibit stronger effects than lower-level; COO connections stronger than CEO/CFO

2. **Exogenous shock analysis**: Departure of connected executive prompts earlier termination of business relations (hazard ratio 2.64×)

3. **Information asymmetry tests**: Effect of personal connections stronger when asymmetry is higher (greater distance, lower accounting quality)

**Consequences Documented:**

1. **Contract provisions**: Connected suppliers face less restrictive procurement contracts (0.74 fewer provisions) and longer contract duration (+6 months)

2. **Operating efficiency**: Customer performance improves after formation of connected supply-chain relationship:
   - Lower inventory volatility
   - Lower cost of goods sold volatility
   - Higher sales efficiency
   - Higher ROA

>[!summary] Overall Contribution
>This study highlights the role of personal connections in:
>- Reducing information asymmetry in supply chains
>- Improving operating efficiency
>- Substituting for formal contracting mechanisms
>- Expanding geographical boundaries of firm operations

## 2. Hypothesis Development

### Customer-Supplier Relationships as Strategic Partnerships

Customer-supplier relationships are widely recognized as an important part of supply-chain management.

>[!info] Strategic Importance
>Sarkis and Talluri (2002): "One of the critical challenges faced by purchasing managers is the selection of strategic partners that will furnish their firms with the necessary products, components, and materials in a timely and effective manner to help maintain a competitive advantage."

**Influence Beyond Product Provision:**

Research documents that customer-supplier relationships affect:
- Firm capital structure (Kale and Shahrur 2007)
- Liquidity management (Cohen and Li 2014, 2017; Costello 2017)
- Financial-reporting policy (Hui et al. 2012)

### Supply-Chain Risks

Supply-chain risks can stem from multiple sources:

**Supplier-Side Risks:**
- Increase in price of supplier inputs or financing costs
- Supplier setback (e.g., plant fire)
- Supplier re-allocation of product capacity due to demand changes
- Supplier bankruptcy

**Mitigation Mechanisms:**

Operations research suggests several ways to mitigate these risks:
- **Operational approaches**: Multiple sourcing, inventory buffers, flexible contracts
- **Contractual approaches**: Performance guarantees, penalty clauses, monitoring provisions

>[!warning] Limitations of Traditional Mitigation
>However, the mechanisms these studies suggest are either:
>- Too costly to implement, or
>- Cannot effectively shield customer from all risks involved
>
>Thus these risks **must be evaluated when selecting a supplier**.

### The Rise of Integrated Supply Chains

In recent years, more firms have moved toward **integrated supply chains** in which larger parts are outsourced (Yang 2009; McCarthy et al. 2013; Boyarchenko and Costello 2015).

**Consequences of This Trend:**
- Accentuated supply-chain risks
- Increased complexity of supplier-selection process
- Greater reliance on softer evaluation measures

>[!example] Soft vs. Hard Evaluation Criteria
>Research (Ittner et al. 1999) argues soft measures better mitigate supply-chain risks than focusing only on price.
>
>**Prominent soft criteria** (Ho et al. 2010 literature review):
>1. Product quality, reputation, position in industry (Rank 1)
>2. Delivery reliability / on-time delivery (Rank 2)
>3. Price / cost (Rank 3)
>4. Manufacturing capability
>5. Management quality
>6. Manufacturing technology / technical capability
>7. Research and development
>8. Financial strength / risk
>9. Production flexibility / response to customer requests

These evaluation measures employed in supplier selection **accentuate the importance of soft information and informal channels** of acquiring information by customers.

### Personal Connections and Soft Information Transmission

Consistent with research showing personal connections improve transmission of soft (private) information between:
- Firms and fund managers (Cohen et al. 2008)
- Lenders and borrowers (Engelberg et al. 2012)
- Headquarters and division managers (Duchin and Sosyura 2013)
- Board members (Fracassi 2017)

This study argues personal connections may also be influential in the supplier-selection process.

**Mechanisms:**

**Before Relationship Formation:**
- Customer executives with personal connections may already have extensive knowledge of connected vendor's operations through informal interactions
- This knowledge likely mitigates information asymmetry
- Helps customer more accurately assess supply-chain risks

**After Relationship Formation:**
- Personal connections reduce costs of monitoring supplier
- Improve effectiveness of monitoring
- Allow customer to receive timely updates of supplier shocks
- Enable timely actions to minimize exposure to supply-chain risks

>[!important] Research Hypothesis
>**H1:** The likelihood that a vendor becomes a supplier increases if personal connections exist between the vendor's and the customer's executives.

## 3. Sample Selection, Identification, and Descriptive Statistics

### 3.1. Sample Selection

**Starting Point:** Compustat Industry Segment file

**Time Period:** 2000-2011 (BoardEx U.S. coverage limited before 2000)

**Initial Criteria:**
- First-time customer-supplier relations among U.S. public firms
- Sales to major customers reported in footnotes under SFAS 14 and SFAS 131
- Customer accounts for ≥10% of reporting firm's total sales (mandatory threshold)

**Exclusions:**
- Voluntary disclosures of customers <10% of sales (non-random reporting concern)
- Suppliers not disclosing identity of major customers

>[!note] Data Limitation
>One drawback of using segment data: dataset includes only large customers (≥10% of supplier's sales). Thus start date of relationship may have been earlier than first disclosure in filings.

**Reverse Causality Mitigation:**

Following Engelberg et al. (2012), focus on two connection types likely to predate business relations:

1. **Educational connections**: Attended same university, overlapped ≥1 year, received same degree (classified into 6 categories: MBA, medical, MA/MS, PhD, law, undergraduate)

2. **Prior work-based connections**: Worked for same employer before employment at current customer and vendor

**Additional Requirements:**
- Personal connections must predate reported start date of business relations by ≥2 years
- Verified with 50 random observations: searched web and Factiva database for first mention of customer-supplier relations—in all 50 cases, personal connection preceded first mention

**Data Merging:**
- Merged with BoardEx database (reports work histories, education, social organizations for C-level executives, lower-level executives, current directors)
- Obtained financial data from Compustat
- Obtained stock return data from CRSP

### 3.2. Identification of Non-Supplier Vendors

**Challenge:** Need to identify potential suppliers that were **not selected** as suppliers (non-supplier vendors)

**Requirement:** Non-supplier vendors must be:
- Comparable to actual supplier
- Realistic candidates for customer to consider

**Cost Consideration:**

Identifying suppliers is very costly for customers, involving:
- Reference checks
- Financial status checks
- Ability to meet specifications assessment
- Product/service quality evaluation

The more vendors considered, the higher the search cost. Thus customers typically consider a **small set of vendors** when selecting supplier.

**Approach:** Following Erel et al. (2019), use **revealed preference** based on attributes of selected supplier

>[!example] Revealed Preference Logic (Erel et al. 2019 Analogy)
>Erel et al. (2019) constructed candidate pool of directors by identifying directors who, within one year of appointment, joined board of smaller neighboring firm of focal firm.
>
>Similarly, this study selects non-supplier vendors within four-digit SIC code of supplier that most resemble selected vendor. **Assumption:** Potential supplier candidates customer considers resemble actual supplier based on revealed preferences.

**Selection Process:**

**Step 1:** Preliminary requirement—firm must have data available on BoardEx

**Step 2:** Use **Mahalanobis distance approach** to identify up to two non-supplier vendors from:
- Same four-digit SIC industry
- Same fiscal year
- Closest to supplier with respect to:
  - **Size** (total assets)
  - **Performance** (ROA)
  - **Sales**

**Results:**
- Some industries (four-digit SICs) have fewer than three firms
- Identified suitable non-supplier vendors for **1,430 suppliers**
- Total of **2,630 non-supplier vendors**
- **Baseline average probability** ≈ 35% that vendor will be selected as supplier

$$\text{Baseline Probability} = \frac{\text{Suppliers}}{\text{Suppliers + Non-suppliers}} = \frac{1430}{1430 + 2630} \approx 0.35$$

>[!tip] Robustness to Matching Procedure
>The study acknowledges focus on three factors (size, ROA, sales) is somewhat exploratory and lacks theoretical support. Customers may consider additional factors when winnowing list of potential suppliers.
>
>**However**, as Table 1 shows, matching based on three factors yields vendors also comparable to actual supplier across multiple other factors. Therefore these three factors seem to parsimoniously encompass firms' underlying fundamentals that customer likely considers.

**Table 1 Insights (Supplier vs. Non-Supplier Vendor Comparison):**

Except for a few variables (supplier gross margin and age), non-supplier vendors and suppliers are **similar in most fundamental characteristics**, suggesting the three variables employed in Mahalanobis distance do good job matching.

**Additional Endogeneity Mitigation:**

To further alleviate concern that firm characteristics might drive results:
- Include all characteristics from Table 1 as control variables in regression analyses
- Include **distance** as control variable (non-supplier vendors slightly farther from customer than suppliers)
- Construct alternative sample matching on distance from customer (eliminates distance difference; results in Table A4 robust)

## 4. Empirical Analyses

### 4.1. The Effect of Personal Connections on Customers' Choice of Suppliers

**Regression Model:**

$$\text{SUPPLIER}_{i,j,t} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{CONNECTED}_{i,j,t} + \beta_2 \text{DISTANCE}_{i,j,t} + \sum_{l} \gamma_l \text{VEND\_CONTROLS}_{l,j,t} + \text{Customer-Year FE} + \epsilon_{i,j,t}$$

**Breakdown of Variables:**

- **$\text{SUPPLIER}_{i,j,t}$** : Indicator = 1 if vendor $j$ is supplier of customer $i$ at year $t$, 0 otherwise
- **$\text{CONNECTED}_{i,j,t}$** : Indicator = 1 if ≥1 educational or work-based connection exists between customer $i$ and vendor $j$, 0 otherwise
- **$\text{DISTANCE}_{i,j,t}$** : $\ln(\text{distance in miles between headquarters})$—controls for delivery time/costs and information gap
- **$\text{VEND\_CONTROLS}_{l,j,t}$** : Large set of vendor $j$ characteristics at time $t$
- **Customer-Year FE** : Customer-year fixed effects (absorb customer-specific controls)
- **Subscripts**: $i$ = customer, $j$ = vendor, $t$ = fiscal year, $l$ = vendor characteristic

>[!info] Why Linear Probability Model (OLS)?
>Ai and Norton (2003) note nonlinear fixed-effects models generate biased estimates for interaction terms. To:
>- Mitigate these biases
>- Enable more intuitive interpretation of economic magnitude
>
>Study focuses on **linear probability model using OLS** (following Duchin and Sosyura 2014). Verified magnitude resembles probit regression.

**Control Variables (Based on Supplier-Selection Literature):**

The large set of vendor controls is based on prominent factors customers consider:

1. **Product quality and reputation**: Age, Sales, Dominance in product market
2. **Delivery reliability**: Distance between customer and vendor
3. **Product price/cost competitiveness**: Gross margin, Operating margin
4. **Manufacturing capability**: Capital expenditure, Asset tangibility
5. **Management quality**: Ratio of executives graduating from top universities
6. **Technology/technical capability**: Ratio of executives with PhDs and master's degrees
7. **Research and development**: R&D intensity
8. **Financial strength/risk**: Cash holdings, Leverage, ROA, Sales growth, Negative cash flow
9. **Production flexibility**: Inventory holding period, PP&E turnover, Days of AR, Days of AP, Days of inventory

#### Table 4: Main Results

**Sample:** N = 4,060 (customer-supplier and customer-non-supplier vendor pairs identified using Mahalanobis distance)

**Alternative Specifications:**

| **Column** | **Connection Measure** | **Coefficient on CONNECTED** | **Interpretation** |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | CONNECTED (indicator, combined) | 0.210*** | 21 percentage point increase |
| 2 | CON_EDU and CON_WORK (separate indicators) | 0.125** (EDU)<br>0.221*** (WORK) | Past-work effect larger |
| 3 | $\ln(1 + \text{# connections})$ (combined) | Positive*** | Continuous measure |
| 4 | $\ln(1 + \text{# EDU})$ and $\ln(1 + \text{# WORK})$ | Positive*** | Continuous measures |

***** p < 0.01, ** p < 0.05, * p < 0.10

**Economic Magnitude (Column 1):**

$$\text{Effect of Connection} = \frac{0.21}{0.35} = 60\% \text{ increase over baseline probability}$$

**Breakdown:**
- **Coefficient**: 0.21 = Personal connection increases probability by 21 percentage points
- **Baseline probability**: 35% (from sample composition: 1430 suppliers / 4060 total)
- **Percentage increase**: 60% = Connection makes selection 60% more likely relative to baseline

>[!example] Interpreting the Magnitude
>**Baseline scenario**: Without personal connections, vendor has 35% chance of being selected
>
>**With personal connections**: Vendor has 35% + 21% = 56% chance of being selected
>
>**Relative increase**: $\frac{21}{35} = 0.60 = 60\%$ increase

**Other Key Findings:**

- **DISTANCE**: Coefficient significantly negative (as predicted—greater distance reduces selection likelihood)
- **Vendor controls**: Largely insignificant (expected because non-supplier vendors similar to suppliers by design)
- **Past-work vs. Educational**: Past-work connections (0.221) show larger effect than educational connections (0.125)

### 4.2. Endogeneity and Sensitivity Analyses

Though comprehensive control variables are included, supplier-selection process involves multiple unobservable factors, raising endogeneity concerns (omitted correlated variables). Additional analyses mitigate these concerns.

#### 4.2.1. The Effect of Management Rank and Position

**Logic:** Effect of personal connections likely varies with:
- **Influence** of connected executive on supplier selection
- **Function** of executive in firm

>[!tip] Why This Mitigates Endogeneity
>To the extent endogeneity exists, it is **unlikely to vary predictably** with rank and position of connected executive. Thus tests of effect across different levels and functions help alleviate endogeneity concern.

**Prediction:**
- Customer **COOs** (typically oversee supplier selection) should have **stronger influence** than other C-level executives
- **C-level** executives should have stronger influence than **lower-level** executives

**Sample Selection for Fair Comparison:**

In each analysis, keep only observations where:
- Specific personal connection in consideration exists, OR
- No connection exists at all

**Example (Column 1, CEO-CEO connections):**
- **Include**: Customer CEOs connected to vendor CEOs OR no connection exists
- **Exclude**: Connections through CFOs, COOs, or other executives

This procedure yields different sample sizes across columns.

>[!summary] Interpretation
>Results comport with expectations:
>- **Effect varies with executive rank**: C-level > Non-C-level; CEO connections strongest
>- **Effect varies with executive position**: COO (who oversees supply chain) shows strongest effect
>
>These predictable variations support causal interpretation and mitigate endogeneity concerns.

#### 4.2.2. Executive Departure Analysis

**Approach:** Identify exogenous shock to personal connection—**departure of connected executive** that severs connection—and test whether it affects duration of business relationship.

**Logic:**

Personal connections facilitate:
- Transmission of timely information outside formal channel
- Alleviation of customer concerns about supply disruption
- Mitigation of moral hazard

**However**, during course of business relations, other employees likely develop trust that may reduce importance of personal connection.

>[!question] Research Question
>Whether departure of connected executive should affect longevity of supply-chain relations is not clear. Can newly developed trust fully substitute for informal communication channel?
>
>**This is an empirical question.**

**Sample Selection:**

- Identify all customer-supplier pairs with **only one C-level executive departing** during supply-chain partnership period
- Focus on single departure to:
  - Increase test power
  - Avoid confounding effects of multiple departures (e.g., poor performance causing both multiple departures and relationship termination)

**Sample Construction:**
- Total C-level departures for full sample: 3,996
- Pairs with single C-level departure: 470
- After data requirements: **308 observations**
- **Unit of analysis**: Executive-customer-supplier level

**COX Proportional Hazard Model:**

$$h(t) = h_0(t) \exp\left(\beta_1 \text{CONNECTED\_EXEC}_{i,j,q,t} + \beta_2 \text{DISTANCE}_{i,j,t} + \beta_3 \text{DURATION\_PRE}_{i,j,t} + \beta_4 \text{CONNECTED\_FIRM}_{i,j,t} + \sum \text{Controls}\right)$$

**Breakdown of Variables:**

- **$h(t)$** : Hazard function—instantaneous rate of relationship termination at time $t$
- **$h_0(t)$** : Baseline hazard function (unspecified)
- **DURATION_POST** : Time in months from executive $q$'s departure to termination of business relation (right-censored if relationship exists at sample end: 78 of 308 censored)
- **CONNECTED_EXEC** : Indicator = 1 if departing executive $q$ is personally connected to counterparty executive, 0 otherwise
- **DURATION_PRE** : Duration in months of supply-chain relationship **before** executive departure (controls for prior relationship length)
- **CONNECTED_FIRM** : Indicator = 1 if pair has **another** personal connection besides departing executive, 0 otherwise (expectation: additional connection mutes departure effect)
- **DISTANCE** : $\ln(\text{distance in miles})$
- **SUPP_CONTROLS** : Supplier controls (similar to supplier-selection analysis)
- **CUST_CONTROLS** : Customer size, age, ROA, stock returns (control for performance)

**Panel A: Descriptive Statistics (N = 308)**

| **Metric** | **Connected Exec Departure** | **Unconnected Exec Departure** | **Difference** |
|---|---|---|---|
| % of departing execs personally connected | 5.5% | 94.5% | — |
| Average duration PRE-departure (months) | 10.5 | 13.2 | Not significant |

>Post-departure duration shows **significant difference**:
>- Connected executive departure: 18 months

>This suggests departure of personally connected executive increases likelihood of business relationship being severed.

>- Alleviates endogeneity concerns related to personal connections

### 4.3. Do Personal Connections Mitigate Information Asymmetries in Supplier Selection?

To further strengthen argument that **mitigating information asymmetry** is the channel through which personal connections affect supplier selection, the study conducts cross-sectional analyses varying degree of information asymmetry.

**Regression Model (Extended):**

$$\text{SUPPLIER}_{i,j,t} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{CONNECTED}_{i,j,t} + \beta_2 \text{INFO\_ASYM}_{i,j,t} + \beta_3 (\text{CONNECTED} \times \text{INFO\_ASYM})_{i,j,t} + \text{Controls} + \text{FE} + \epsilon$$

**Prediction:**

If personal connections improve customer's knowledge about connected vendor and reduce information asymmetry, then:

$$\beta_3 > 0$$

**Interpretation:** Relation between personal connections and likelihood of becoming supplier will be **stronger when ex ante information asymmetry is higher**.

#### Information Asymmetry Proxies

**1. Geographic Distance (DISTANCE)**

>[!info] Why Distance Proxies Information Asymmetry
>Research finds evidence that proximity to suppliers reduces information asymmetry because of:
>- Lower costs of communicating soft information (Noordewier et al. 1990; Cannon and Homburg 2001)
>- Lower monitoring costs (Hortacsu et al. 2009; Costello 2013)

**Measurement:** $\ln(\text{distance in miles between headquarters})$

**2. Accounting Quality (ABN_ACCRUALS_S)**

>[!info] Why Accounting Quality Proxies Information Asymmetry
>Financial information represents important source of **hard information**. High accounting quality can mitigate information asymmetry between firm and outsiders (Wittenberg-Moerman 2008; Ball et al. 2008; Beatty et al. 2010; Chen et al. 2015).
>
>Financial information allows customers to assess **default risk** of supplier, critical to procurement decisions (Babich et al. 2007; Beil 2010).

**Measurement:** Following Aboody et al. (2005), measure as **absolute value of abnormal accruals**—residual from modified Jones model (Dechow et al. 1995)
- **Higher abnormal accruals** = Lower accounting quality = **Higher information asymmetry**

#### Table 7: Cross-Sectional Test Results

**Sample:** N = 4,060 (baseline sample)

| **Column** | **Info Asymmetry Proxy** | **CONNECTED** | **INFO_ASYM** | **CONNECTED × INFO_ASYM** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | DISTANCE | 0.056*** | −0.018*** | **0.025***  |
| 2 | ABN_ACCRUALS_S | 0.158*** | −0.062 (n.s.) | **0.364*** |

**Economic Magnitude:**

**Distance (Column 1):**

$$\text{One SD increase in DISTANCE} = 1.522$$
$$\text{Increase in effect of connections} = 0.025 \times 1.522 = 0.038$$
$$\text{Percentage increase} = \frac{0.038}{0.056} = 68\%$$

**Breakdown:**
- **Baseline effect of CONNECTED** (at mean distance): 0.056 (5.6 percentage points)
- **One standard deviation increase in distance**: 1.522 (log miles)
- **Additional effect from interaction**: 0.025 × 1.522 = 0.038
- **Total effect at high distance**: 0.056 + 0.038 = 0.094 (9.4 percentage points)
- **Percentage increase**: 68% = $(0.094 - 0.056) / 0.056$

**Abnormal Accruals (Column 2):**

$$\text{One SD of ABN\_ACCRUALS\_S} = 0.186$$
$$\text{Increase in effect of connections} = 0.364 \times 0.186 = 0.068$$
$$\text{Percentage increase} = \frac{0.068}{0.158} = 43\%$$

>[!summary] Interpretation
>Results suggest **importance of personal connections to supplier selection increases with information asymmetry**:
>- **Greater geographic distance** (harder to communicate soft info, monitor): Personal connections matter **68% more**
>- **Lower accounting quality** (poorer hard information): Personal connections matter **43% more**
>
>These results suggest personal connections facilitate **soft-information transmission** between supply-chain partners, thereby:
>- Expanding firms' geographical boundaries
>- Mitigating frictions arising from poor-quality hard information

### 4.4. Consequences of Personal Connections in the Supply Chain

To better depict effects of personal connections, the study investigates consequences of existence of personal connections between customer and supplier.

#### 4.4.1. The Effect of Personal Connections on Provisions in Procurement Contracts

**Theoretical Background:**

Contractual terms in procurement contracts are commonly used to address counterparty risk from:
- **Adverse selection**: Customer doesn't know supplier's true quality
- **Moral hazard**: Supplier may shirk or cut corners after contract signed

**Common Contractual Provisions:**
- Product warranties
- Customer's right to inspect supplier's plants
- Supplier-paid liability or property insurance
- Pre-scheduled periodic meetings between parties

>[!info] Information Asymmetry and Contract Complexity
>- **Hasija et al. (2008)**: When firms face high information asymmetry, contract documents are **longer and more complex** to mitigate risks from uncertainty and ambiguity
>- **Costello (2013)**: Contract duration **decreases** with information asymmetry; covenants more **restrictive** when monitoring cost is high

**However**, contractual provisions are costly to implement:
- Require effort, time
- May require payments to third parties (e.g., inspectors, insurers)

**Predictions:**

If personal connections mitigate information asymmetries:
1. Customers impose **fewer restrictions** on connected suppliers
2. Contract duration **increases** with personal connections (Costello 2013)

**Sample Construction:**

**Step 1:** Broad search using 10-K wizard tool for all material contracts (2000-2011) with title containing:
- "Supply contract" or "supply agreement"
- "Procurement contract" or "procurement agreement"
- "Service(s) contract" or "service(s) agreement"
- **Initial hits**: 6,899 contracts

**Step 2:** Merge with BoardEx data → **3,135 contracts**

**Step 3:** Match counterparty names; eliminate contracts amending earlier contract

**Step 4:** Firms often redact commercially sensitive information (e.g., contract duration per Verrecchia and Weber 2006)
- **Exclude redacted contracts**
- **Final sample**: **154 unique contracts**

**Step 5:** Read each contract and extract:
- Duration
- Restrictive provisions

**Regression Model (Overall Restrictiveness):**

$$\text{RESTRICTIVENESS}_{i,j,t} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{CONNECTED}_{i,j,t} + \beta_2 \text{DURATION}_{i,j,t} + \beta_3 \text{EXCLUSIVITY}_{i,j,t} + \sum \text{SUPP\_CONTROLS} + \sum \text{CUST\_CONTROLS} + \text{Year FE} + \text{Supplier Industry FE} + \epsilon$$

**Breakdown of Variables:**

- **RESTRICTIVENESS** : Index = Number of provisions imposing cost/penalty on supplier (range 0–6)
  - **Provisions counted**:
    1. Product warranty (WARRANTY)
    2. Supplier-paid liability or property insurance (INSURANCE)
    3. Right to inspect supplier's production facilities (INSPECTION)
    4. Pre-specified meetings between parties (MEETING)
    5. Third-party certification of deliverables
    6. Third-party performance guarantee
  - **Higher values** = More restrictive contracts

- **DURATION** : Shortest possible length in years allowed by contract (following Costello 2013)

- **EXCLUSIVITY** : Indicator = 1 if contract includes exclusivity term (makes seeking alternative suppliers harder)

- **SUPP_CONTROLS** : Supplier size, profitability, leverage, asset tangibility, Altman Z-score

- **CUST_CONTROLS** : Customer size

- **Fixed Effects**: Year FE, Supplier Industry FE (control for time-trend and time-invariant industry differences in acceptable contractual terms)

>[!note] Why Not Customer Fixed Effects?
>Few customers have multiple procurement contracts in sample:
>- Out of 154 customers: 120 have single contract, 12 have ≥2 contracts
>- Insufficient variation for customer FE

**Regression Models (Individual Provisions and Duration):**

For contract duration and individual restrictive provisions, estimate system of seemingly unrelated equations:

$$\text{DURATION}_{i,j,t} = f(\text{CONNECTED}, \text{PROV}_{1-4}, \text{Controls})$$
$$\text{PROV}_{k,i,j,t} = f(\text{CONNECTED}, \text{OTHER\_PROV}_{g \neq k}, \text{DURATION}, \text{Controls})$$

where $k \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ = WARRANTY, INSURANCE, INSPECTION, MEETING

**Rationale for Seemingly Unrelated Equations:**

Contract terms are likely **jointly determined**. However, coming up with multiple exogenous instruments to jointly estimate all terms is empirically prohibitive. Following Costello (2013), use OLS to estimate in system of **five seemingly unrelated equations** (SUR).

#### 4.4.2. The Effect of Personal Connections on Customer Operations

To further probe consequences of doing business with personally connected suppliers, the study examines whether customer-supplier relationships likely created via personal connections affect **customer operating efficiency** following formation of business relationship.

**Four Alternative Measures of Operating Efficiency:**

1. **INV_STD_C**: Volatility of inventory (measures inventory-supply interruptions)
2. **COGS_STD_C**: Volatility of cost of goods sold (measures inventory overstocking/write-downs)
3. **SALE_ASSET_C**: Sales efficiency = Sales / Assets
4. **ROA_C**: Return on assets (overall operating performance)

**Rationale:**

**First two proxies** measure efficiency of **inventory management**, likely capturing supply-chain efficiency directly:

- **Fewer supply disruptions** → Lower volatility of inventory (if personal connections improve monitoring and information flow)
- **Fewer disruptions** → Less fluctuation in product costs + less inventory hoarding → Lower likelihood of write-downs → Lower volatility of COGS

**Third and fourth proxies** measure **overall firm efficiency**, indirectly affected by supply chain.

**Sample:** Restricted to **actual customer-supplier pairs** (not non-supplier vendors)

**Event Study Design:**

$$\text{OUTCOME\_C}_{i,j,t} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{CONNECTED}_{i,j,t} + \beta_2 \text{POST}_t + \beta_3 (\text{CONNECTED} \times \text{POST})_{i,j,t} + \sum \text{CUST\_CONTROLS} + \text{Customer FE} + \epsilon$$

**Breakdown of Variables:**

- **OUTCOME_C** : One of four operating efficiency measures (INV_STD_C, COGS_STD_C, SALE_ASSET_C, ROA_C)
- **POST** : Indicator = 1 for years **after** formation of supply-chain relationship, 0 otherwise (focuses on fiscal year in which relationship starts)
- **CONNECTED** : Indicator = 1 if personal connection exists
- **CONNECTED × POST** : **Variable of interest**—captures differential effect of connected supplier on customer efficiency post-relationship formation
- **CUST_CONTROLS** : Book-to-market ratio, Leverage, Tangibility, Age, Gross margin (following Feng et al. 2015)
- **Customer FE** : Customer fixed effects

**Estimation:** Use **weighted least squares (WLS)** to account for large customers (e.g., Walmart) with multiple suppliers in sample
- **Weighting**: Each observation weighted by **inverse of number of suppliers** of that customer in sample
- **Rationale**: Count each customer once; each supplier weighted by relative impact on customer; avoids heteroskedasticity problems
- **Effect**: WLS reduces number of observations compared to summary statistics

**Panel A: Descriptive Statistics (Customer-Year Level)**

|---|---|
| Sample span | 12 years |
| % of firm-years with connections | 17.5% |

>[!note] Why Lower Percentage?
>17.5% with connections (firm-year level) is **lower than** 28.4% for customer-supplier pairs (Table 3).
>
>**Reason:** Dilution from including customer firm-years **before** formation of connected supply-chain relationship, in which case CONNECTED = 0.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | INV_STD_C (inventory volatility) | Not significant | **Negative*** | Connected suppliers reduce volatility |
| 2 | COGS_STD_C (COGS volatility) | Not significant | **Negative*** | Connected suppliers reduce volatility |
| 3 | SALE_ASSET_C (sales efficiency) | Not significant | **Positive*** | Connected suppliers improve efficiency |
| 4 | ROA_C (return on assets) | Not significant | **Positive*** | Connected suppliers improve performance |

**Interpretation of Coefficients:**

**POST (alone):**
- **Not significant** across all specifications
- **Implication**: Firm operating efficiency **does not change** after transacting with **unconnected supplier** (baseline effect)

**CONNECTED × POST (interaction):**
- **Negative and significant** for volatility measures (INV_STD_C, COGS_STD_C)
  - Personal connections **reduce** inventory volatility and COGS volatility
  - Consistent with fewer supply disruptions, better coordination

- **Positive and significant** for efficiency measures (SALE_ASSET_C, ROA_C)
  - Personal connections **improve** sales efficiency and ROA
  - Consistent with better supplier quality, fewer operational issues

>[!summary] Interpretation of Operating Efficiency Results
>Results not only corroborate main findings (personal connections mitigate information asymmetry in supply chain) but also **highlight real value** of personal connections in improving firm's operational efficiency.
>
>**All four measures improve** after formation of connected supply-chain relationship:
>1. **Lower inventory volatility**: Fewer supply interruptions
>2. **Lower COGS volatility**: Less overstocking, fewer write-downs
>3. **Higher sales efficiency**: Better product flow, fewer stockouts
>4. **Higher ROA**: Overall performance improvement
>
>**Alternative interpretation rejected:** Results suggest role of personal connections in supplier choice is **less likely due to "currying favor"** motive (found in political connections context).
>
>**Reason:** Under currying-favor view, would observe **lower** operating efficiency following formation of personally connected supply chain (selecting inferior suppliers for personal benefit). Evidence shows **opposite**—efficiency improves.

### 4.5. Additional Analyses and Sensitivity Tests

The study conducts multiple sensitivity analyses to provide high level of confidence in interpretation of empirical evidence. Some reported in manuscript, others in **online appendix**.

#### Online Appendix Analyses Summary

**Mitigating Endogeneity (Omitted Correlated Variables):**

1. **Alternative sample: Propensity-score matching** (Table A2)
   - Matches firms according to likelihood of connectedness
   - Ensures connected and unconnected firms comparable on observables

2. **Alternative sample: Distance matching** (Table A4)
   - Eliminates difference in distance between supplier-customer and non-supplier-customer pairs
   - Addresses concern that distance is correlated omitted variable

3. **Additional fixed effects** (Table A3)
   - Supplier-selection analysis including various additional FE
   - Controls for unobservable time-invariant or time-varying factors

**Results:** Robust to all three approaches

**Mitigating Reverse Causality:**

4. **Extended time gaps** (Table A5)
   - Increases required time gap between start of personal connection and start of business connection
   - **From 2 years** → up to **25 years** in 5-year increments
   - Ensures connections truly predate business relations

**Results:** Robust across all time gaps

**Addressing Empirical Choices:**

5. **Alternative matching: 3 or 4 non-supplier vendors** (Table A6)
   - Instead of 2 non-supplier vendor matches
   - Tests sensitivity to matching ratio

6. **Alternative sample: All four-digit SIC firms** (Table A7)
   - Includes all firms in same four-digit SIC as non-supplier vendors
   - Instead of matched subsample
   - More exhaustive comparison set

7. **Alternative industry classification: Text-based** (Table A8)
   - Uses 10-K text-based fixed industry classification (Hoberg and Phillips 2010, 2016)
   - Instead of SIC codes
   - Addresses concern that SIC may not capture true industry peers

**Results:** Insensitive to these alternative empirical choices

**Cross-Sectional Analysis:**

8. **Multiple vendors in close proximity** (Table A9)
   - Tests whether effect of personal connections more pronounced when **few vendors in close proximity**
   - **Logic**: Greater information asymmetry when fewer local vendors available
   - **Result**: Effect of personal connections **more pronounced** when information asymmetry greater (few nearby vendors)
   - Lends further credence to main findings

**Addressing Sample Selection:**

9. **Heckman two-step procedure** (Table A10)
   - For procurement-contract analysis
   - Addresses sample-selection issue (not all suppliers have observable contracts)
   - **Stage 1**: Model likelihood of entering contract sample
   - **Stage 2**: Model contract provisions conditional on being in sample

**Results:** Intact after controlling for selection

>[!tip] Overall Robustness
>The extensive battery of sensitivity tests provides high confidence that:
>- Results not driven by specific empirical choices
>- Endogeneity concerns substantially mitigated
>- Reverse causality unlikely explanation
>- Information-asymmetry channel supported across multiple specifications

## 5. Conclusion

### Summary of Main Findings

This study examines the role of personal connections in selection of suppliers. Key findings:

**1. Personal Connections Increase Supplier Selection Likelihood**

A customer is more likely to choose as supplier a vendor to whom its executives are personally connected than vendor without such connections.

- **Magnitude**: 60% increase over baseline probability (21 percentage points on 35% baseline)
- **Connection types**: Effect holds for both educational and prior employment connections
- **Stronger effect**: Past-work connections (0.221) > Educational connections (0.125)

**2. Effect Varies Predictably Across Management**

- **By rank**: C-Level connections > Non-C-Level connections; CEO-CEO strongest
- **By position**: COO connections (0.472) > CEO (0.302) > CFO (0.234)
- Variation consistent with influence on supplier-selection process

**3. Robustness to Endogeneity Tests**

- Executive departure analysis: Loss of connection increases termination hazard 2.64×
- Multiple sensitivity analyses address omitted variables, reverse causality
- Results robust to alternative samples, specifications, time gaps

**4. Effect Stronger When Information Asymmetry Higher**

- **Greater geographic distance**: Personal connections matter 68% more
- **Lower accounting quality**: Personal connections matter 43% more
- Consistent with information-mitigation channel

**5. Personal Connections Affect Contract Provisions**

- **Less restrictive contracts**: 0.74 fewer provisions (29% reduction)
- **Longer duration**: +6 months
- **Lower likelihood**: Warranty, inspection, meeting requirements
- Suggests informal relationships substitute formal contracting

**6. Personal Connections Improve Customer Operating Efficiency**

All four measures improve after formation of connected supply-chain relationship:
- **Lower inventory volatility**: Fewer supply interruptions
- **Lower COGS volatility**: Better inventory management
- **Higher sales efficiency**: Better product flow
- **Higher ROA**: Overall performance improvement

>[!important] Rejection of Currying-Favor Hypothesis
>Results suggest role of personal connections in supplier choice is **less likely due to currying favor** (selecting inferior suppliers for personal benefit).
>
>**Evidence**: Operating efficiency **improves** (not deteriorates) after connected supplier selection, inconsistent with favoritism.

### Contributions to Literature

**1. Personal Connections in Operating Decisions**

Highlights importance of personal connections in critical firm operating decision (supplier selection), extending literature beyond investing and financing decisions.

**2. Soft Information Transmission**

Highlights role of personal connections in:
- Transmitting soft information
- Expanding geographical constraints along supply chains
- Complementing research on fund managers' investments (Cohen et al. 2008) and firms' external financing (Engelberg et al. 2012)

**3. Real Value Creation**

Shows personal connections:
- Relax procurement-contract terms
- Improve firms' operating efficiency

Corroborates evidence that personal connections alleviate informational frictions and offers insights into:
- **Contracting literature**: Informal relationships can substitute formal contracts
- **Real effects**: Personal connections create tangible operational value (not just selection effects)

### Contribution to Supply Chain Literature

Adds to emerging literature examining role of social connections in addressing information frictions and agency (Sapienza 2004; Engelberg et al. 2012; Duchin and Sosyura 2012).

Evidence in supply-chain setting comports with personal connections **mitigating information frictions**, extending understanding to:
- Operating relationships (not just financial)
- Bilateral partnerships (customer-supplier)
- Ongoing relationships (not just one-time transactions)

### Study Limitations

>[!warning] Acknowledged Limitations
>
>**1. Public Company Focus**
>Study focuses on supply-chain relationships in **public company domain**. Given that private firms are more informationally opaque, conceivable that personal connections might play:
>- Different role among private firms
>- Even more important role (due to greater information asymmetry)
>
>**2. Remaining Endogeneity Concerns**
>As noted earlier, findings should be interpreted with caution since study might not have fully addressed all possible endogenous issues.
>
>**Specific concerns:**
>- Unobservable factors affecting both connection formation and supplier selection
>- Non-supplier vendor selection based on revealed preference (subjective choice)
>- Cannot rule out all alternative explanations despite extensive testing

### Practical Implications

**For Managers:**
- Personal connections provide **real operational value** in supply-chain relationships
- COOs and senior executives overseeing supply chain should leverage existing connections
- Building relationships with potential suppliers (e.g., through industry associations, board service) may facilitate future partnerships
- However, connections should complement (not replace) rigorous supplier evaluation

**For Investors:**
- Personal connections between customer-supplier executives may signal:
  - Higher-quality supplier relationships
  - Improved operational efficiency
  - Lower supply-chain disruption risk
- Consider executive turnover implications for supply-chain stability

**For Policymakers:**
- Personal connections can reduce transaction costs and improve efficiency
- However, need to balance benefits against potential for:
  - Reduced competition (if connections create barriers to entry for unconnected vendors)
  - Conflicts of interest (if executives benefit personally from supplier selection)
- Disclosure requirements for connected relationships may enhance transparency

>[!summary] Overall Conclusion
>This study highlights that **personal connections help reduce transaction costs** in supplier selection by:
>- Mitigating information asymmetries
>- Facilitating trust and open communication
>- Enabling more effective monitoring and coordination
>- Creating real operational value for customers
>
>Personal connections between customer and supplier executives represent an **informal governance mechanism** that complements formal contracting and produces measurable improvements in operational efficiency.
>
>The evidence is consistent with personal connections serving a **productive role** (information mitigation) rather than purely **rent-seeking behavior** (currying favor), though limitations remain in fully ruling out all endogenous factors.
