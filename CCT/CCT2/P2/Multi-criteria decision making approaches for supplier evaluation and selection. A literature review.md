## 1. Introduction

### The Evolution of Supplier Selection

>[!important] Contemporary Supply Management Paradigm Shift
>Contemporary supply management aims to maintain **long-term partnerships with suppliers** and use **fewer but reliable suppliers**. Therefore, choosing the right suppliers involves much more than scanning price lists—choices depend on **wide range of factors involving both quantitative and qualitative** considerations.

**Traditional vs. Modern Approach:**

| **Traditional Approach** | **Modern Multi-Criteria Approach** |
|---|---|
| Single criterion: **Lowest cost bidding** | Multiple criteria: Quality, delivery, cost, capability, service, etc. |
| Transactional relationships | Long-term partnerships |
| Many suppliers for competition | Fewer, more reliable suppliers |
| Quantitative focus only | Quantitative **and** qualitative factors |

### Multi-Criteria Decision Making (MCDM) Methods Overview

Extensive MCDM approaches have been proposed for supplier selection:

**Individual Methods:**
- **Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP)**
- **Analytic Network Process (ANP)**
- **Case-Based Reasoning (CBR)**
- **Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA)**
- **Fuzzy Set Theory**
- **Genetic Algorithm (GA)**
- **Mathematical Programming**
- **Simple Multi-Attribute Rating Technique (SMART)**

**Integrated Methods (Hybrids):**
- AHP + Goal Programming (GP)
- AHP + DEA
- Fuzzy + AHP
- ANP + Multi-objective Programming
- And many other combinations

### Previous Literature Reviews

At least three journal articles have reviewed supplier evaluation and selection models:

1. **Weber et al. (1991)**: Literature review through 1991
2. **Degraeve et al. (2000)**: Review through 2000
3. **De Boer et al. (2001)**: Review through 2000

>[!note] Gap in Literature
>Since these articles review literature up to **2000**, this paper **extends them** by surveying multi-criteria supplier evaluation and selection approaches from **2000 to 2008**.

### Research Scope and Questions

**Sample:** 78 journal articles collected from international journals (2000-2008)
- **Databases searched**: Emerald, Ingenta, MetaPress, ProQuest, ScienceDirect

**Three Research Questions:**

1. **Which approaches were prevalently applied?**
2. **Which evaluating criteria were paid more attention to?**
3. **Is there any inadequacy of the approaches?**

Based on identified inadequacies, improvements and possible future work are recommended.

## 2. Individual Approaches

### 2.1. Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA)

**Prevalence:** 14 out of 78 articles (17.95%)—**most popular individual approach**

>[!info] What is DEA?
>DEA is a linear programming technique used to measure **relative efficiencies of homogeneous decision-making units (DMUs)**. In supplier selection context, DEA evaluates suppliers as DMUs based on multiple inputs and outputs.
>
>**Basic Concept:** Suppliers generating **more outputs while requiring less input** are regarded as more efficient suppliers.

#### Key DEA Applications in Supplier Selection

**Braglia and Petroni (2000)**
- Based on Baker and Talluri (1997) work
- Applied DEA to measure efficiencies of alternative suppliers
- **Nine evaluating factors** to measure supplier rating
- Used **cross-efficiency** and **Maverick index** to avoid selecting sub-optimal or "false positive" suppliers

**Liu et al. (2000)**
- Simplified DEA model
- Evaluated overall supplier performance with **3 inputs + 2 outputs**
- **Objective**: Select supplier with higher supply variety → reduce total number of suppliers needed

**Forker and Mendez (2001)**
- Measured comparative efficiencies of suppliers
- For each supplier: calculated **maximum ratio of single input to multiple outputs**
- Outputs based on critical factors of quality management from literature
- Used **cross-efficiencies** to find "best peer" suppliers (similar to Braglia and Petroni 2000)

**Narasimhan et al. (2001)**
- Applied DEA for multinational telecommunications corporation
- **11 evaluating factors**:
  - **6 inputs**: Supplier capability metrics
  - **5 outputs**: Supplier performance metrics
- **Classification scheme**: Based on performance score, suppliers categorized into four groups:
  1. High performers and efficient
  2. High performers and inefficient
  3. Low performers and efficient
  4. Low performers and inefficient

**Talluri and Baker (2002)**
- **Three-phase approach** for logistics distribution network design
- **Phase I**: Evaluated potential stakeholders (suppliers, manufacturers, distributors) individually using DEA
- **6 evaluating factors**: 2 inputs + 4 outputs (from literature)
- **Phase II**: Determined optimal number of stakeholders to utilize
- **Phase III**: Identified optimal routing of material (suppliers → manufacturers → warehouses)

**Talluri and Sarkis (2002)**
- Measured supplier performance using DEA
- Same evaluating factors and dataset as Talluri and Baker (2002)
- Focused on illustrating how model works

**Talluri and Narasimhan (2004)**
- Applied DEA for effective supplier sourcing
- Similar to Narasimhan et al. (2001) but with methodological differences:
  - **Narasimhan et al. (2001)**: Used simple efficiency scores
  - **Talluri and Narasimhan (2004)**: Used **cross-efficiencies + statistical methods** for categorizing supply base into clusters

**Garfamy (2006)**
- Applied DEA based on **total cost of ownership concept**
- Supplier providing single unit of output charging **least amount of costs** = most efficient

**Ross et al. (2006)**
- Evaluated supplier performance with respect to **both buyer and supplier performance attributes**
- **Three sensitivity analyses**:
  1. Compute efficiency scores **without** evaluation team's weights and bounds
  2. Consider evaluation team's preferences on **supplier performance attributes**
  3. Consider buyer's preferences on **supplier performance attributes**

**Saen (2006)**
- Evaluated technology suppliers with **3 factors** (including 1 qualitative: amount of know-how transfer)
- Used **five-point scale** to rank suppliers on qualitative factor

**Seydel (2006)**
- DEA with **no input considered** (unusual approach)
- Used **seven-point scale** for qualitative criteria ratings
- **Claimed advantage**: Required less involvement of decision makers than SMART

**Talluri et al. (2006)**
- Presented **chance-constrained DEA** for stochastic performance measures
- **Input**: Price
- **Outputs**: Quality, Delivery
- Compared with deterministic DEA to highlight usefulness

**Saen (2007a)**
- Presented **imprecise DEA** for quantitative + qualitative data
- **Example qualitative output**: Supplier reputation (SR)—cannot be quantified legitimately
- Model allowed decision makers to provide **complete rank ordering** of suppliers on SR
- Could handle **fuzzy data** in forms of bounded data

**Wu et al. (2007)**
- Presented **augmented imprecise DEA**
- **Two capabilities**:
  1. Handle imprecise data (rank efficient suppliers)
  2. Increased discriminatory power (discriminate efficient from poor performing suppliers)
- Developed **web-based system** for potential buyers for supplier evaluation and selection

>[!summary] DEA Evolution in Supplier Selection (2000-2008)
>**Early DEA (2000-2002):**
>- Numerical data only
>- Deterministic models
>- Focus on efficiency scores and cross-efficiency
>
>**Advanced DEA (2006-2008):**
>- Handle **qualitative data** (five-point/seven-point scales)
>- Handle **stochastic performance** (chance-constrained DEA)
>- Handle **imprecise/fuzzy data** (imprecise DEA, augmented imprecise DEA)
>- **Web-based systems** for implementation
>
>**Key Advantage**: Robustness and ability to adapt to complex, real-world supplier selection problems

### 2.2. Mathematical Programming

**Prevalence:** 9 papers (11.54%)—**second most popular individual approach**

Mathematical programming formulates supplier selection as optimization models with various structures.

#### 2.2.1. Linear Programming (LP)

**Talluri and Narasimhan (2003)**
- **First researchers** considering **performance variability measures** in evaluating suppliers
- Developed **two LP models**:
  1. **Maximize performance** against best target measures set by buyer
  2. **Minimize performance** against best target measures
- **Rationale**: Measuring both max and min efficiencies → comprehensive understanding of supplier performance

**Talluri and Narasimhan (2005)**
- LP model to evaluate/select potential suppliers with respect to **strengths of existing suppliers**
- **Objective**: Exclude underperforming suppliers from telecommunications company's supply base
- Compared with traditional and advanced DEA to examine relative advantages

**Ng (2008)**
- **Weighted linear programming model**
- **Objective**: Maximize supplier score
- Similar to AHP: involves decision makers in determining **relative importance weightings of criteria**

#### 2.2.2. Integer Linear Programming (ILP)

**Talluri (2002)**
- **Binary integer linear programming**
- Evaluated alternative supplier bids based on **ideal targets for bid attributes** set by buyer
- Selected optimal set of bids by matching **demand and capacity constraints**
- Proposed **effective negotiation strategies** for unselected bids (based on four model variations)

**Hong et al. (2005)**
- **Mixed-integer linear programming**
- Determined:
  1. Optimal **number** of suppliers
  2. Optimal **order quantity**
- **Objective**: Maximize revenue
- Considered change in suppliers' supply capabilities and customer needs **over time**

#### 2.2.3. Integer Non-Linear Programming (INLP)

**Ghodsypour and O'Brien (2001)**
- **Mixed integer non-linear programming**
- Solved multi-criteria sourcing problem
- Determined **optimal allocation of products to suppliers**
- **Objective**: Minimize total annual purchasing cost
- **Three constraints** considered in model

#### 2.2.4. Goal Programming (GP)

**Karpak et al. (2001)**
- Constructed GP model to evaluate and select suppliers
- **Three goals**: Cost, Quality, Delivery reliability
- Determined **optimal amount of products ordered**
- **Constraints**: Buyer's demand, Supplier's capacity

#### 2.2.5. Multi-Objective Programming

**Narasimhan et al. (2006)**
- Multi-objective programming model
- Selected optimal suppliers and determined optimal order quantity
- **Five criteria** to evaluate supplier performance
- **Process**: Derive relative importance weightings of criteria **before** solving model (suggested AHP as possible method)

**Wadhwa and Ravindran (2007)**
- Modeled as multi-objective programming problem
- **Three objective functions**: Minimize (price, lead time, rejects)
- **Three solution approaches** compared:
  1. Weighted objective method
  2. Goal programming method
  3. Compromise programming

>[!example] Mathematical Programming Summary
>**Common Features Across Mathematical Programming Approaches:**
>- **Optimization focus**: Find optimal supplier set and/or order quantities
>- **Constraint consideration**: Demand, capacity, budget constraints
>- **Multiple objectives**: Cost, quality, delivery, revenue, etc.
>
>**Types by Complexity:**
>- **Simplest**: Linear Programming (3 papers)
>- **Moderate**: Integer Linear Programming (2 papers), Goal Programming (1 paper)
>- **Complex**: Integer Non-Linear Programming (1 paper), Multi-Objective Programming (2 papers)

### 2.3. Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP)

**Prevalence:** 7 papers (8.97%)—**third most popular individual approach**

>[!info] What is AHP?
>AHP is a structured technique for organizing and analyzing complex decisions based on mathematics and psychology. It represents and quantifies decision elements, relates those elements to overall goals, and evaluates alternative choices through **pairwise comparisons**.
>
>**Key Feature**: **Consistency verification** acts as feedback mechanism for decision makers to review and revise judgments, guaranteeing consistent judgments (basic ingredient for good decisions).

#### Key AHP Applications

**Akarte et al. (2001)**
- Developed **web-based AHP system** for casting supplier evaluation
- **18 criteria** for evaluation
- **Process**:
  1. Suppliers register and input casting specifications
  2. Buyers determine relative importance weightings for criteria (based on casting specs)
  3. Buyers assign performance rating for each criterion using **pairwise comparison**

**Muralidharan et al. (2002)**
- Proposed **five-step AHP-based model**
- Aided decision makers in rating and selecting suppliers
- **9 evaluating criteria**
- **Multi-functional involvement**: People from purchasing, stores, quality control departments

**Chan (2003)**
- Developed **interactive selection model with AHP**
- **"Interactive"**: Incorporated **chain of interaction method** to determine relative importance of criteria **without subjective human judgment**
- AHP only applied to generate **overall score** for alternative suppliers based on relative importance ratings (not for determining weights)

**Chan and Chan (2004)**
- Applied AHP to evaluate and select suppliers
- **AHP hierarchy**: 6 evaluating criteria + 20 sub-factors
- Relative importance ratings computed based on **customer requirements**

**Liu and Hai (2005)**
- Applied AHP to evaluate and select suppliers
- Similar to Chan (2003): did **not** apply AHP's pairwise comparison for relative importance ratings
- Instead: used **Noguchi's voting and ranking method**—allowed every manager to **vote or determine order** of criteria instead of weights

**Chan et al. (2007)**
- AHP-based decision making approach
- Evaluated suppliers based on **14 criteria**
- **Sensitivity analysis** using Expert Choice software: examined response of alternatives when relative importance rating of each criterion changed

**Hou and Su (2007)**
- Developed **AHP-based decision support system** for supplier selection in **mass customization environment**
- Considered factors from **external and internal influences** to meet needs of markets within global changing environment

>[!tip] AHP Variations Observed
>**Traditional AHP** (full process):
>- Pairwise comparisons for criteria weights
>- Pairwise comparisons for alternative ratings
>- Consistency checking
>- Overall score calculation
>
>**Modified AHP** (hybrid approaches):
>- **Chain of interaction method** instead of subjective pairwise comparison (Chan 2003)
>- **Noguchi's voting/ranking** instead of pairwise comparison for weights (Liu and Hai 2005)
>- **Customer requirements-based** weighting (Chan and Chan 2004)

### 2.4. Case-Based Reasoning (CBR)

**Prevalence:** 7 papers (8.97%)—**tied third with AHP**

>[!info] What is CBR?
>CBR is a problem-solving paradigm that utilizes specific knowledge of previously experienced, concrete problem situations (cases). A new problem is solved by finding a similar past case and reusing it in the new problem situation.
>
>**In Supplier Selection Context**: CBR retrieves/selects supplier who best meets specification predefined by company based on **previous successful and relevant cases**.

#### Key CBR Applications

**Choy and Lee (2002)**
- Presented **generic model using CBR technique**
- **Evaluating criteria grouped into 3 categories**:
  1. Technical capability
  2. Quality system
  3. Organizational profile
- **Implementation**: Consumer products manufacturing company
- **Database system**: Stored performance of past suppliers and their attributes
- **Selection process**: Model retrieves supplier who **most closely meets predefined specification**

**Choy et al. (2002, 2003a, 2003b, 2004a, 2005)** and **Choy and Lee (2003)**
- Applied CBR-based model again (six additional papers)
- Very similar approach to Choy and Lee (2002), including supplier selection workflow
- Deployed to **same company** as Choy and Lee (2002)
- **Only difference**: Supplier evaluating factors varied across studies (see Appendix 4)

>[!note] CBR Research Pattern
>Notably, all 7 CBR papers come from **same research group** (Choy and colleagues), deployed in **same company**, with very similar methodology. This suggests:
>- CBR approach is **less widely adopted** across different research groups
>- May be more company-specific or context-dependent
>- Represents iterative refinement by single research team rather than broad adoption

### 2.5. Analytic Network Process (ANP)

**Prevalence:** 3 papers (3.85%)

>[!info] What is ANP?
>ANP is a generalization of AHP. While AHP represents a framework with a **unidirectional hierarchical relationship**, ANP allows for **complex interrelationships** among decision levels and attributes through a **network structure**.
>
>**Key Advantage**: Captures **interdependencies and feedback** among criteria that AHP cannot model.

#### Key ANP Applications

**Sarkis and Talluri (2002)**
- Believed supplier evaluating factors **influence each other**—**internal interdependency** needed in evaluation
- Applied ANP to evaluate and select best supplier
- **Evaluating criteria**: Organizational factors + strategic performance metrics (7 criteria total)
- **Impact consideration**: How these factors affect each other

**Bayazit (2006)**
- Proposed ANP model for supplier selection
- **10 evaluating criteria** classified into **2 clusters**:
  1. Supplier's **performance** cluster
  2. Supplier's **capability** cluster
- **Formulating interrelationships**: Each criterion considered as **controlling factor** for pairwise comparison matrix

**Gencer and Gürpinar (2007)**
- Implemented ANP model in **electronic company**
- Evaluated and selected most appropriate supplier
- **Criteria classification**: 3 clusters
- **Interrelationships** among criteria considered in selection process

>[!example] ANP vs. AHP Comparison
>**When to use AHP:**
>- Criteria are **independent**
>- Hierarchical structure appropriate
>- Simpler problem
>
>**When to use ANP:**
>- Criteria have **interdependencies** or **feedback loops**
>- Network structure needed (e.g., quality affects delivery, delivery affects cost, cost affects quality)
>- More complex problem requiring richer modeling
>
>**Trade-off**: ANP provides richer modeling but is more complex and time-consuming than AHP

### 2.6. Fuzzy Set Theory

**Prevalence:** 3 papers (3.85%)

>[!info] What is Fuzzy Set Theory?
>Fuzzy set theory deals with reasoning that is approximate rather than fixed and exact. It allows for **partial membership** in a set (values between 0 and 1) rather than binary membership (0 or 1).
>
>**In Supplier Selection**: Handles **imprecision** and **subjectivity** in expert assessments, particularly for qualitative criteria that cannot be precisely quantified.

#### Key Fuzzy Applications

**Chen et al. (2006)**
- Presented **hierarchy model based on fuzzy-sets theory**
- Used **linguistic values** to assess ratings and weights for supplier evaluating factors
- **Linguistic ratings** expressed in:
  - **Trapezoidal fuzzy numbers**, or
  - **Triangular fuzzy numbers**
- **Capability**: Handle both **quantitative and qualitative criteria**

**Sarkar and Mohapatra (2006)**
- Suggested **performance and capability** as two major measures in supplier evaluation
- Used fuzzy set approach to account for **imprecision involved in numerous subjective characteristics** of suppliers
- **Hypothetical case**: Selected two best suppliers
- **Evaluation factors**:
  - **4 performance-based factors**
  - **10 capability-based factors**

**Florez-Lopez (2007)**
- Picked **14 most important evaluating factors** from **84 potential added-value attributes**
- Based on questionnaire response from **US purchasing managers**
- Used **two-tuple fuzzy linguistic model** to combine **numerical and linguistic information**
- **Graphical output**: Relative suitability of suppliers + identification of strategic groups of suppliers

>[!tip] Fuzzy Set Theory Advantage
>**Main benefit**: Represents **vagueness and imprecision** more realistically than crisp numbers.
>
>**Example**: Instead of saying "Supplier quality = 8 out of 10" (crisp), fuzzy allows:
>- "Supplier quality is approximately between 7 and 9, most likely 8" (triangular fuzzy: 7, 8, 9)
>- Captures uncertainty in expert judgment

### 2.7. Simple Multi-Attribute Rating Technique (SMART)

**Prevalence:** 2 papers (2.56%)

>[!info] What is SMART?
>SMART is a compensatory MCDM method that assigns importance weights to criteria and rates alternatives on each criterion. The overall score for each alternative is calculated as the **weighted sum of its ratings**.
>
>**"Simple"**: Less complex than AHP (no pairwise comparisons), more straightforward for decision makers.

#### Key SMART Applications

**Barla (2003)**
- Conducted **five-step approach based on SMART**
- **Application**: Supplier evaluation and selection in **glass manufacturing company**
- **7 evaluating criteria** with **multiple sub-factors**
- **Selection rule**: Subcontractor with highest score (called "total expected utilities") selected

**Huang and Keska (2007)**
- Presented comprehensive set of **101 metrics** collected from literature
- Instead of using all metrics: selected **relevant criteria and metrics** for selection process
- **Practical approach**: Emphasized not all metrics need to be used—choose appropriate subset

>[!note] SMART vs. AHP
>**SMART advantages:**
>- Simpler, more straightforward
>- Less time-consuming (no pairwise comparisons)
>- **Seydel (2006)** addressed: DEA required less involvement of decision makers than SMART
>
>**SMART disadvantages:**
>- No built-in consistency check (unlike AHP)
>- Weights assigned more subjectively without pairwise comparison discipline

---

### 2.8. Genetic Algorithm (GA)

**Prevalence:** Only **1 paper (1.28%)**—**least popular individual approach**

>[!info] What is GA?
>GA is a search heuristic inspired by Charles Darwin's theory of natural evolution. It reflects the process of natural selection where the fittest individuals are selected for reproduction to produce offspring of the next generation.
>
>**In Supplier Selection**: GA evolves solutions (supplier configurations) over multiple generations, selecting better configurations based on "fitness" (performance on key indicators).

#### Key GA Application

**Ding et al. (2005)**
- Presented **GA-based optimization methodology** for supplier selection
- **Provided**: Possible **configurations of selected suppliers**, including **transportation modes**
- **Evaluation**: Each configuration evaluated with respect to **key performance indicators**
- **Process**: GA evolves configurations over generations, keeping better-performing configurations

>[!warning] Limited Adoption of GA
>Only 1 out of 78 papers (1.28%) used GA as **standalone** approach. However, GA appears more frequently in **integrated approaches** (combined with ANN, fuzzy, multi-objective programming).
>
>**Possible reasons for limited standalone use:**
>- GA is optimization heuristic, not evaluation framework—better suited as **optimization component** within larger framework
>- Requires defining fitness function and encoding, which may be complex for supplier selection
>- Other methods (DEA, AHP, mathematical programming) more intuitive for decision makers

## 3. Integrated Approaches

### 3.1. Integrated AHP Approaches

**Prevalence:** 14 papers (17.95%)—**tied with DEA as most popular overall approach**

>[!important] Why Integrate AHP?
>AHP possesses unique advantages:
>- **Simplicity, ease of use, great flexibility** (Ho, 2008)
>- **Consistency verification** prevents inconsistency through feedback mechanism
>- Guarantees **consistent judgments**—basic ingredient for good decisions
>
>**However**, AHP output is **only relative importance weightings** of criteria/sub-factors. In supplier selection, decision makers also need to consider:
>- **Resource limitations** (budget of buyer, capacities of suppliers)
>- **Optimal order quantities**
>- **Allocation decisions**
>
>**Solution**: Integrate AHP with other techniques that compensate for these limitations.

  1. **AHP**: Measure relative importance weightings of potential suppliers

**Kull and Talluri (2008)**

**Mendoza et al. (2008)**
- **5 evaluating criteria**

  1. Reduce large number of potential suppliers to **manageable number**

>[!summary] Why AHP-GP is Most Popular Integrated Approach
>**AHP advantages:**
>- Consistency verification prevents inconsistent judgments

>
>**GP advantages:**
>- Handles **resource limitations** (budget, capacity)
>- Determines **optimal order quantities**

>
>**Combined strength**: AHP provides reliable weightings → GP optimizes allocation decisions considering constraints
>
>**Result**: More complete decision support—not just "which suppliers are best" but "how much to order from each supplier given constraints"

### 4.3. Limitations of Approaches

**Third Objective**: Critically analyze approaches and find drawbacks

**Focus**: Analysis confined to **two most popular approaches**:
1. **DEA** (most popular individual approach)
2. **AHP-GP** (most popular integrated approach)

Reasons for popularity found in Section 4.1.

#### Limitations of DEA

**Three major limitations/drawbacks:**

**1. Confusion with Input and Output Criteria**

Practitioners may be confused about whether criterion should be input or output.

>[!example] Price/Cost Classification Inconsistency
>**As OUTPUT criterion** (want to maximize—unusual):
>- Narasimhan et al. (2001)
>- Talluri and Narasimhan (2004)
>- Seydel (2006)
>
>**As INPUT criterion** (want to minimize—more common):
>- Liu et al. (2000)
>- Narasimhan et al. (2001)—*same authors, inconsistent!*
>- Talluri and Baker (2002)
>- Talluri and Sarkis (2002)
>- Talluri and Narasimhan (2004)—*same authors, inconsistent!*
>- Garfamy (2006)
>- Ross et al. (2006)
>- Saen (2006)
>- Saen (2007a)
>- Talluri et al. (2006)
>- Wu et al. (2007)
>
>**Problem**: No clear consensus on proper classification—creates confusion for practitioners

**2. Subjective Assignment of Ratings to Qualitative Criteria**

Although Saen (2006) and Seydel (2006) deployed **five-point and seven-point scales** to rank qualitative criteria priorities, **inconsistencies may occur** because of subjective judgments.

>[!warning] Subjectivity Problem
>**Issue**: Even with rating scales, different experts may assign different ratings to same qualitative criterion (e.g., "supplier reputation", "management quality")
>
>**No consistency check**: Unlike AHP, DEA has no built-in mechanism to verify consistency of subjective judgments

**3. Efficiency vs. Effectiveness Question**

DEA is linear programming measuring **relative efficiencies** of homogeneous DMUs. Suppliers generating **more outputs while requiring less input** = more efficient suppliers.

>[!question] Critical Question Raised
>"Is an **efficient** supplier equivalent to an **effective** supplier?"
>
>**Efficiency**: Doing things right (maximizing output/input ratio)
>**Effectiveness**: Doing the right things (achieving desired outcomes)
>
>A supplier may be **efficient** (low cost per unit delivered) but **ineffective** (delivering wrong products, poor quality despite efficiency metrics)

#### Limitations of Integrated AHP-GP Approach

**Process Recap:**
1. **AHP**: Determine relative importance weightings of alternative suppliers (multiple criteria)
2. **GP**: Incorporate weightings into model → determine optimal supplier set + optimal order quantity (subject to resource constraints)

**One Potential Problem: AHP Consensus Time**

>[!warning] Time-Consuming Consensus in AHP
>May be **time-consuming in reaching consensus**. Decision makers must:
>
>1. **Compare each cluster** in same level in pairwise fashion (based on experience/knowledge)
>2. **At second level**: Compare every two criteria with respect to goal
>3. **At third level**: Compare every two sub-factors of same criteria with respect to corresponding criterion
>
>**Consistency issue**: If consistency ratio **exceeds limit**, decision makers must **review and revise pairwise comparisons again**—iterative process can be lengthy
>
>**Example**: For 10 criteria, requires $\frac{10 \times 9}{2} = 45$ pairwise comparisons. If inconsistent, all 45 must be reviewed/revised.

**Additional Considerations:**

While not explicitly stated as "limitations" by paper, integrated AHP-GP also faces:
- **Complexity**: Requires expertise in both AHP and GP
- **Software requirements**: May need specialized software (Expert Choice for AHP, optimization software for GP)
- **Training needs**: Decision makers need training in both methodologies

5. **Greater awareness** of supply chain's strategic importance

## 5. Future Work

### Current Limitation: Missing "Voice" of Company Stakeholders

>[!warning] Critical Gap in Current Approaches
>Although above-mentioned approaches can deal with **multiple and conflicting criteria**, they have **not taken into consideration**:
>- Impact of **business objectives** on evaluating criteria
>- **Requirements of company stakeholders** on evaluating criteria
>
>**Reality**: Weightings of supplier evaluating criteria depend **a lot on business priorities and strategies**.

**Problem with Arbitrary Weighting:**

In cases where weightings assigned **arbitrarily and subjectively** without considering **"voice" of company stakeholders**, the suppliers selected may **not provide what company exactly wants**.

>[!example] Stakeholder Voice Example
>**Scenario**: Company stakeholders (marketing, operations, finance, customers) have different priorities:
>- **Marketing**: Wants suppliers who can support new product launches (flexibility, innovation)
>- **Operations**: Wants reliable delivery and consistent quality
>- **Finance**: Wants cost reduction
>- **Customers**: Want fast delivery and customization
>
>**Traditional approach**: Weights assigned arbitrarily by purchasing manager
>**Problem**: May not reflect actual needs of all stakeholders
>**Result**: Selected supplier may excel in areas stakeholders don't care about, fail in areas they do

### Proposed Solution: Integrated AHP-QFD Approach

To enable **"voice" of company stakeholders** to be considered:

>[!tip] Recommended Future Research Direction
>An **integrated analytical approach** combining **AHP and QFD** should be developed to select suppliers **strategically**.

**Proposed Process:**

**Step 1: QFD—Derive Evaluating Criteria**
- Multiple evaluating criteria derived from **requirements of company stakeholders**
- Use **series of house of quality** (QFD structure)

**Step 2: QFD + AHP—Prioritize Criteria**
- **Importance of evaluating criteria** prioritized with respect to **degree of achieving stakeholder requirements**
- Use **AHP** for prioritization (consistent pairwise comparisons)

**Step 3: AHP—Evaluate Alternative Suppliers**
- Based on ranked criteria, alternative suppliers **evaluated and compared** with each other
- Use **AHP** again to make optimal selection

#### How QFD Contributes

**Most Important Information QFD Provides:**

**Importance weightings of evaluating criterion** derived by:
1. **Importance ratings** of stakeholder requirements
2. **Relationship weightings** between stakeholder requirements and evaluating criterion

>[!example] QFD House of Quality Structure
>```
>                    Evaluating Criteria
>                    (Quality, Delivery, Cost, ...)
>                    ↓        ↓        ↓
>Stakeholder    →   [Relationship Matrix]  → Importance
>Requirements                                  Weightings
>(Fast delivery,
> Low defects,
> Flexibility, ...)
>```
>
>**Relationships** show how well each criterion satisfies each requirement
>**Weightings** calculated based on importance of requirements × strength of relationships

#### Problem with Traditional QFD

**Generally**:
- **Importance ratings** of stakeholder requirements: Determined by decision makers **arbitrarily**
- **Relationship weightings**: Determined by decision makers **arbitrarily**

**Result**: May result in **certain degree of inconsistency** → **degrade quality of decisions made**

#### Solution: Integrate AHP into QFD

**To overcome drawback**: Use **AHP** to evaluate both importance ratings and relationship weightings **consistently**.

**AHP Advantage**: Built-in **consistency verification** ensures:
- Judgments are logically consistent
- Inconsistencies identified and corrected through iterative feedback

**However**: Proposed **AHP–QFD approach has not been applied** to supplier selection problem yet (as of 2008).

>[!important] Research Gap and Opportunity
>**Gap**: Despite theoretical promise, integrated AHP-QFD approach for supplier selection **not yet implemented** in literature (as of 2008)
>
>**Opportunity**: Future researchers should:
>1. Develop detailed AHP-QFD methodology for supplier selection
>2. Validate approach through case studies
>3. Compare performance with existing approaches (AHP-GP, DEA, etc.)
>4. Develop software tools to facilitate implementation

## 6. Conclusions

This paper conducted **literature review on multi-criteria decision making approaches for supplier evaluation and selection from 2000 to 2008**.

### Key Findings Summary

**1. Numerous Individual and Integrated Approaches Proposed**

All approaches capable of handling **multiple quantitative and qualitative factors**.

**Most Prevalent Approaches:**

| **Category** | **Most Popular** | **Papers** | **Percentage** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Individual Approach | **DEA** | **14** | **17.95%** |
| Integrated Approach | **AHP-GP** | **6** | **~8%** |

**2. Price/Cost NOT Most Popular Criterion**

>[!important] Paradigm Shift Confirmed
>**Most popular criterion** for evaluating supplier performance:
>1. **Quality** (68 papers, 87.18%)
>2. **Delivery** (64 papers, 82.05%)
>3. **Price/Cost** (63 papers, 80.77%)
>
>**Proves**: Traditional **single criterion approach based on lowest cost** is **not supportive and robust enough** in contemporary supply management.

**Why Traditional Cost-Based Approach Insufficient:**

The traditional cost-based approach **cannot guarantee** selected supplier is **globally optimal** because:
- **Customer-oriented criteria** (quality, delivery, flexibility, service) **not considered**
- Focuses only on **efficiency** (low cost) not **effectiveness** (achieving desired outcomes)
- Ignores **long-term relationship** factors (capability, technology, management)
- Missing **strategic considerations** (innovation, risk, sustainability)

**3. Recommendations Made Based on Inadequacies**

**For DEA:**
- Clarify input/output classification guidelines
- Develop consistency checks for subjective qualitative ratings
- Distinguish between efficiency and effectiveness in supplier evaluation

**For AHP-GP:**
- Develop methods to reduce time for consensus
- Create software tools to facilitate pairwise comparisons
- Consider integrating with QFD to capture stakeholder voice

**For Future Research:**
- Develop integrated AHP-QFD approach
- Validate through case studies
- Compare with existing approaches

### Contributions of This Review

>**1. Evidence for Multi-Criteria Approaches**

### Practical Implications

**For Practitioners:**
- **Don't rely solely on cost** when selecting suppliers
- **Consider multiple criteria**: Quality, delivery, capability, service, etc.
- **Use appropriate MCDM method** based on:
  - Problem complexity (individual vs. integrated approach)
  - Data availability (quantitative vs. qualitative)
  - Resource constraints (time, expertise)
  - Stakeholder requirements

**For Researchers:**
- **Build on existing approaches**: Don't reinvent the wheel—integrate proven methods
- **Address identified limitations**: Input/output confusion in DEA, consensus time in AHP, etc.
- **Develop new integrations**: Particularly AHP-QFD for stakeholder voice
- **Validate empirically**: More case studies needed

### Looking Forward

The **importance of supplier selection to effective supply chain** ensures continued growth in this research area. Future developments likely to focus on:

1. **Integration with Industry 4.0**: Big data, AI, IoT for real-time supplier evaluation
2. **Sustainability considerations**: Environmental and social criteria gaining importance
3. **Risk management**: Supply chain resilience after disruptions (e.g., COVID-19)
4. **Dynamic evaluation**: Real-time performance monitoring vs. periodic evaluation
5. **Stakeholder alignment**: Better integration of diverse stakeholder requirements

>[!important] Final Takeaway
>**Multi-criteria decision making approaches have proven superior to traditional cost-based supplier selection.** The shift from cost-only to multi-criteria evaluation reflects the evolution of supply management from **transactional purchasing** to **strategic partnership**—a trend that will only intensify as supply chains become more complex, global, and critical to competitive advantage.
